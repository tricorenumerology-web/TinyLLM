The Keeper of Lantern Rock

Once upon a time, on a small grey island called Lantern Rock, there lived a keeper named Miro. Every evening Miro climbed the round stone stairs of the lighthouse, lit the great lamp, and turned it toward the sea. The light swept the water once, twice, three times, and the ships far away knew they were safe.

Miro lived in a little white house beside the lighthouse. He had a blue boat, a warm stove, three shelves of old books, and a cat named Biscuit. Biscuit was orange and round and slept most of the day in a patch of sun. In the morning Miro drank tea and watched the gulls. In the afternoon he mended the nets and painted the railings. In the evening he climbed the stairs and lit the lamp. That was the whole of his life, and he loved it.

One windy night, the storm came early. The rain hit the windows like small stones. The wind shook the lighthouse, and the lamp flickered. Miro held the flame with his two hands until the wind grew tired and went away. In the morning the beach was full of treasure from the sea: green glass, gray rope, a boot, and something else.

The something else was a small wooden box, round and heavy, with an iron lock. Miro carried it home and set it on the table. Biscuit sniffed it once and sat down in front of it, very serious, like a small orange guard.

"Miro, what is in the box?" asked the box, in a voice like a creaking stair.

Miro nearly dropped his tea. "Boxes do not talk," he said.

"This one does," said the box. "I am a wishing box. Open my lock and make one wish. But be careful, keeper. A wish is like the sea. It gives you what you ask for, and also what you did not ask for."

Miro thought about this all day. He mended the nets and thought. He painted the railings and thought. He climbed the stairs, lit the lamp, and thought while the light swept the water once, twice, three times.

He did not wish for gold. Gold is heavy and you cannot drink it. He did not wish for a big house. A big house has many windows to clean. He did not wish for a fast ship. He already had a blue boat, and the blue boat knew the way home.

At sunrise, Miro opened the lock with the small key from his grandmother's drawer, and he said: "I wish for enough."

The box was quiet for a long time. Then it laughed, a warm sound, like a kettle. "Enough," it said. "That is the rarest wish of all. Most people wish for more. Nobody wishes for enough. Your wish is granted, keeper."

And it was. The hens laid enough eggs. The rain gave enough water. The stove burned enough wood. The books were enough stories. Biscuit caught enough mice and slept enough hours in the sun. When the fishing boats passed Lantern Rock in the evening, the keeper's light was strong and steady, and the sailors said, there goes the richest man on the coast, though his house was small and his boat was old and his pockets were empty.

Years later, when Miro was very old and his beard was white as foam, a girl knocked at his door. She had rowed all the way from the mainland in a green boat. "Are you the keeper of Lantern Rock?" she asked. "They say you once owned a wishing box."

"I did," said Miro. "It is under the stairs. It is yours now, if you want it. But first, drink some tea. Then tell me, what will you wish for?"

The girl thought while the tea steamed and Biscuit slept. "I wish for a friend," she said at last.

"Good," said Miro, and he smiled. "But I must tell you a secret. The box was empty all along. The wishing was ours. We did it ourselves, with the tea and the table and the quiet morning."

The girl looked at the box, and the box looked at the girl, and neither one said a word. Outside, the sun came over the water, and the gulls turned gold for a moment, and it was enough.

That is the story of the keeper of Lantern Rock. Tell it to someone you like, with a cup of tea, on a windy evening, and make a wish of your own. Wish for enough.

More Tales from Lantern Rock

The Fog

One morning the fog came to Lantern Rock and did not leave. It was a thick white fog, the kind you could butter on bread. Miro could not see the sea, the gulls, or his own blue boat. He could see only the first three steps of the stairs, and then white.

"How will the ships find the way?" asked Biscuit, who talked on foggy days only.

"They will listen," said Miro. And he rang the old bell, slow and deep, once, twice, three times. Under the white sea of fog, the sound rolled like a fish. The ships heard the bell and the sailors smiled, because a bell is a lighthouse for the ears.

By noon the sun ate the fog, bite by bite, and the world came back: the grey rock, the green water, the little white house, the orange cat. Everything was where it had always been. That is the trouble with fog. It hides the world, but it never steals it.

The Visitor

One day a bird knocked on the window of the little white house. It was a small grey bird with a red scarf, and nobody wears a red scarf, so Miro opened the window.

"Good morning," said the bird. "I have flown over nine islands and my wings are tired. May I rest on your windowsill?"

"Rest as long as you like," said Miro. He put out a saucer of water and three crumbs of bread. The bird drank, and ate, and looked at the sea.

"Why did you fly over nine islands?" asked Miro.

"To see them," said the bird, as if it were the most simple answer in the world. "The world is large and I am small, but I have all morning."

The bird rested until the lamp was lit, then flew off into the pink sky. Miro watched it go and thought that this was also a kind of wealth: to be small, and to have all morning.

Winter

In winter the sea turned the colour of iron, and the wind came from the north with cold teeth. Miro wore two sweaters and a hat that his grandmother had knitted long ago. Biscuit wore his own fur and sat closer to the stove.

The storms in winter are long. The lamp burned every night, and Miro carried the oil up the round stone stairs, one can at a time. Sometimes the waves jumped so high that they licked the window, and the whole house hummed like a bottle.

But the winter also brought quiet gifts. The sky at night was full of more stars than anyone could count. The sea went calm as glass between the storms. And on the coldest evening of the year, Miro made soup, and read an old book, and Biscuit slept on his feet, and the light went around and around over the dark water, keeping everyone safe, the way it always had.
